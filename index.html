Four ways
to catch πwithout measuring a curve

π is the length around a circle of diameter 1. No ruler can measure a curve exactly, so mathematicians trapped π instead: between polygons, with random dots, with falling needles, and with an endless sum of fractions. Watch each short film, then try the method yourself.

Before you watch

Four facts the films rely on.

C = πd

Circumference is π times the diameter. With d = 1, the circumference is π itself.

A = πr²

The area of a circle. With r = 1, the area is π.

P = part ÷ whole

For a random point, a probability is a ratio of areas.

sin 30° = ½

Sine and tangent of 30° and 60°, used in film one.

01

c. 250 BC · Archimedes of Syracuse

Trap π between two polygons

A shape inside the circle is too short. A shape outside is too long. π has to sit in between.

Archimedes started with hexagons, then kept doubling the number of sides: 12, 24, 48, 96. Each step squeezes the gap. With 96 sides he proved π lies between 3.1408 and 3.1429.

The idea in one lineinner < π < outer

After watching: Liu Hui (AD 263) went to 3072 sides for 3.1416, and Zu Chongzhi pinned π between 3.1415926 and 3.1415927.

02

1940s · Los Alamos

Throw random dots at a square

A circle of radius 1 covers π⁄4 of the square around it. Random dots find that fraction for you.

Throw dots anywhere in the square and count how many land inside the circle. Four times that fraction heads towards π. It works for any shape, but it is slow: each extra digit costs a hundred times more dots.

The idea in one lineπ ≈ 4 × insideall dots

After watching: the typical error is about 1.64 ÷ √n. Throw 100 dots a few times and see how much the answer jumps.

03

1777 · Georges-Louis Leclerc, Comte de Buffon

Drop needles on a striped floor

There is no circle in sight, yet π decides how often a falling needle crosses a line.

Lines are drawn d apart and a needle of length l lands at random. Because the needle can point in any direction, the crossing probability is 2l ⁄ (πd). Count needles and crossings, flip the formula, and you have π.

The idea in one line (l = d)π ≈ 2 × needlescrossings

After watching: shorten the needle in the lab. It crosses less often, yet the estimate still heads to π.

04

c. 1400 Madhava · 1674 Leibniz

Add up fractions, forever

No circle, no chance. Just odd numbers: add a third, take a fifth… and π appears.

Each new term pushes the sum past π, then back again, and every swing is smaller than the last. The reason is an area: the curve y = 1 ⁄ (1 + x²) encloses exactly π⁄4 between 0 and 1. The catch is speed: the error is about 1 ⁄ n, so each new digit costs ten times more terms.

The idea in one lineπ = 4 × ( 1 − 13 + 15 − 17 + … )

After watching: turn on "averaged sums" in the lab. Averaging two neighbouring sums cancels most of the swing.

Finale · all four methods at once

Four methods, one race

Give every method the same budget: 10,000 steps. Who gets closest to π?

A step is one more side for the polygons, one more dot, one more needle, or one more term of the series. What decides the race is how fast each error shrinks as the steps pile up. On the chart below both axes are logarithmic, so each law becomes a straight line and a steeper line means a faster method.

Try this: run the race a few times. The green and red lines (dots and needles) change every run because they use chance. The polygons and the series give the same answer every time.

Geometry wins the race

The polygon error falls like 1 ÷ n², so doubling the sides makes it four times smaller. With 10,000 sides Archimedes' method already has seven correct decimals.

Chance is honest but slow

Dots and needles improve like 1 ÷ √n, so a hundred times more effort buys one digit. Their value is that the same idea measures areas and probabilities no formula can reach.

Series are how we really do it

The Leibniz series is slow, but it opened the door to faster ones. The Chudnovsky series used by today's record computations adds about 14 correct digits with every term.
